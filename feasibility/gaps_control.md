# Feasibility gaps: control, software, perception and autonomy

Reviewed 2026-10-13. Sources: `home-cooking-robot/` (README, DECISIONS, `sim/r10/claude/README_R10.md`, `R19_*.md`, `results/r19/hub/*.json`, `checker.py`, `meal16.py`, `research/r15_cooking_market.md`, `research/r19_system_cost.md`) and `build_day/` (`cook_api.py`, `chef_agent.py`, `claude_client.py`, `cook_model.py`, `logs/claude_calls.jsonl`, `runs/run0*/run.json`, `out/wok_cam_calibration.md`). Nothing in either folder was changed.

## Summary
1. The geometric motion work is strong and honestly labelled. Everything *above and below* it is either an R19 proposal (monitors, recovery, perception grids are explicitly "[proposal], not checked") or missing. That covers runtime software, perception, state estimation, failure handling, safety monitoring and testing.
2. **Biggest technical gap:** the checker treats the wheeled cart as an exact prismatic joint (`checker.py` l.58, `R_carriage_x`). Yet 15 of the 22 hub free moves keep only 2.0–2.3 mm reserve while the cart drives up to 0.85 m (`results/r19/hub/edge_*.json`), and the lid set keeps 0.3 mm. Hub matching asks for 0.5 mm of cart accuracy (R19_MOTION_LIBRARY §4). No localisation, docking-accuracy or kitchen-registration design exists to deliver this.
3. **The LLM demo freezes the world while Claude thinks.** `chef_agent.py` advances time only inside skills. The ledger shows a median of 9.9 s per decision (p90 12.6 s, max 21.6 s, 118 decisions). In run01, 590 s of thinking sat beside 643 s of kitchen time and was never simulated. The sim's own heat model gives +17 °C per 6 s at full power.
4. **Perception is specified only for the wok, and only in the sim.** The wok camera images are rendered from the hidden truth by the same author who tuned the thresholds (`wok_cam_calibration.md`), so the vision results are circular. IR on oil, steam and a seasoned wok is not characterised; the demo itself returns `None` on a dry wok because the emissivity is not trusted. No cameras or methods are defined for trays, cups, plates, the sink, the fridge box or spills.
5. The contact tasks (scrape stir, lever cutter, fridge door, faucet) are planned as position-replayed paths, but the sim stir used a compliant weld. There is no force-control design, no testing plan (URSim, hardware-in-the-loop, staged bring-up) and no software line in the cost study. All of these can be closed with modest laptop work plus about ¥5–15k of bench hardware (not counting a borrowed or rented arm) before any full-system purchase.

Status legend: COVERED = designed and checked; PARTLY = designed or proposed but not checked, or covered only in the demo; MISSING = not addressed. Effort is for one engineer.

## Top risks

**1. The kitchen and the cart are not where the stored paths think they are.** The whole R19 approach (check offline, replay exactly, no geometry at runtime) is only safe if the real robot base and every fixture sit within each set's clearance reserve. Those reserves are 2.0–2.3 mm on most hub free moves, 2.0 mm in the stir, 2.4 mm at the wok grasp and 0.3 mm at the lid rack. Meanwhile the base is a 65 kg cart on two drive wheels and casters, modelled as an ideal joint and asked to match hubs to 0.5 mm, and it moves *during* actions. Neither a localisation method, nor a docking accuracy, nor a kitchen-registration procedure exists, and the real kitchen is L-shaped while the sim is straight. Close it first, on a laptop, with a tolerance Monte-Carlo (replay all stored sets under perturbed base and fixture poses). Then do a bench repeatability test of the cart with and without indexed stops. If the tolerated error is under about 2 mm, the design needs indexed hard stops or a floor rail with the cart parked during every action, plus larger reserves (re-plan with a 15 mm gap instead of 10). This could invalidate the current 10 mm / 2 mm planning margins, so it gates the rest of the motion work.

**2. An LLM-per-decision loop is too slow for wok cooking as currently modelled.** The demo looks good (run01 and run02 pass every hidden-truth check, peak 147 °C). But it assumes the kitchen stops while Claude thinks for about 10 s per turn, which nearly doubles the dish's wall time (590 s of thinking vs 643 s of cooking in run01). It also ignores that a pause at full power gains about 17 °C in 6 s. Skills like `stir(90 s)` are blind for their whole duration. A 3–5 day laptop re-run with time advancing during calls will show how much breaks. The likely fix is architectural: deterministic temperature and cue controllers at sensor rate inside the skills, with the LLM moved to the meal-planning and exception layer, or used offline to compile recipes into checked skill programs (#24). That keeps the demo's strength (reading recipes and judging cues) without putting a 10–20 s cloud round-trip inside a 5-second thermal loop.

**3. Perception is barely specified, and the wok vision evidence is circular.** The recipes need 157 camera cues and 40 thermometer cues. The only designed sensors are a colour and a thermal camera over the wok. Their demo images are synthetic renders of the hidden truth, scored with thresholds tuned to the same renderer. IR on a thin oil film or a seasoned carbon-steel wok, through steam and smoke, can easily be off by tens of degrees, and the demo already returns `None` on a dry wok. Outside the wok there are no cameras, methods or accuracy targets for tray pick poses, cup and plate presence, the faucet running, spills (9–19 % of rigid dice miss the plate) or tool identity. Close it with a per-skill perception matrix (laptop, 1 week). Then run two cheap bench campaigns: thermal accuracy against a reference thermocouple on a real induction wok, and real hood-camera video of 3 dishes run through the same Claude prompt.

**4. Contact tasks and failures are handled by replaying positions.** Stirring scrapes the steel, the lever cutter needs an unsourced force, and the fridge door and faucet lever are pushed. All are executed as stored position trajectories, while the sim stir relied on a compliant weld. With millimetre-level pose uncertainty (risk 1), a stiff position path either misses the wok or jams it, and a protective stop mid-tip with 1 kg of hot food has no designed safe state. The failure list (R19 §4) is a 6-row proposal and explicitly not checked. It omits dropped food, a stuck lever, an unseated wok, a spatula rotating in the grip and an empty pump, and its "retreat along the stored path" assumes the path is still clear after a drop. Needed: an FMEA per skill with detectors and safe-hold states, sim fault injection, and a bench test of force-mode or passively compliant scraping and lever pulls on a real arm (3–5 weeks with hardware).

**5. Runtime safety with a person sharing the aisle and with hot loads is unanalysed.** The design depends on the user entering during operation: clearing finished plates, loading rice, being in a small L-kitchen aisle that the cart also uses. Yet the presence sensors, separation distances, stopping distance with a loaded wok and the pause frequency are all undefined. Power and force limiting does not address the real hazards here (hot oil, a hot wok, a spatula edge), and a knob servo holds full power if the PC or LLM process dies. Two cheap actions remove most of the exposure. First, a hardware heartbeat watchdog that cuts cooker power (1 week). Second, a hazard analysis with ISO/TS 15066 separation calculations plus a measured UR5e stop with the wok load (2–3 weeks). The analysis also decides which functions must use the arm's safety-rated I/O rather than the PC.
