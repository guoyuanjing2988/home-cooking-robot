# Gap review: mechanical, structure and physical properties

Scope: the R10–R19 tethered-cart design in `home-cooking-robot/` (read-only review, 2026-10-13). Paths are relative to the repo root. In the "status" column, *covered* means derived and evidenced in the repo, *partly* means modelled with assumed constants or only flagged, and *missing* means I found nothing. Numbers marked **[est]** are my own hand calculations, not repo content.

## Summary
1. The cart is a set of guessed rigid MuJoCo boxes, not a design. The "63–67 kg" is the sum of per-geom masses in `scene.py` (structure ≈ 37.8 kg + UR5e 21.0 + tool 1.2 ≈ 60 kg, plus the held load). There is no CAD, no BOM-derived mass, CoG or inertia, no brakes, and no structural check.
2. **The clearances are mm-level, but there is no tolerance budget.** The checked paths keep 2.0–3.4 mm of reserve beyond the 10/5 mm gates (lid 0.3–1.0 mm). The repo treats the cart pose as exact. The only docking spec (R09: ±2 mm, ±0.2°, ≈5 mm at the TCP) is an unmeasured requirement, and the R10+ bumper dock gives no x or yaw reference at all.
3. Tip-over is a quasi-static ZMP screen with unsourced constants (bumper 250 N, μ 0.5, ×1.5 factor). The R17 chains were checked with `mass=0`. R19's "42.7 mm margin at 0.5 m/s²" leaves only about 30 N·m of moment reserve **[est]**, which an arm protective stop holding the 2.95 kg wok could plausibly use up. That case is not modelled.
4. The real kitchen is L-shaped with the sink on the other leg. The sim is a straight 2.94 m run. A cart corner turn carrying a hot wok, the tether routing, the aisle width, the parking spot and the floor slope or thresholds are all missing. The constraint that kills a straight-counter cart concept is unexamined.
5. Thermal, material and duty issues at the wok are only partly covered: printed PETG/TPU fingers on a grip block about 6.5 cm from a 250 °C wok rim, an unmeasured plume, an IP54 arm under the running tap and in grease, a PGC used at 92 % of its *static* moment rating, stir spikes of 8–25 N·m, and no fatigue or wear assessment.

## Top risks

**1. The tolerance stack is larger than the reserves (rows 3, 5, 6, 13, 14).** This is the R07/R08 failure (sub-mm margins) coming back in a different form. The checked paths keep 2.0 mm (stir), 2.4 mm (wok grasp), 3.3 mm (sink) and 0.3–1.0 mm (lid) beyond the 10/5 mm gates. The library treats the cart x as "exact" and relies on fixtures to locate the *objects*, not the *robot*. Several errors add up against those reserves, each an estimate:
- the bumper dock gives no x or yaw reference;
- a 0.2° yaw error alone is 3 mm at full reach;
- tyre rocking could add 2–6 mm, varying with the load;
- the kitchen itself is unmeasured;
- servo tracking error is unmodelled.

The gates themselves (10/5 mm) are the only real allowance, and nobody has allocated them. To close this, write the budget, then re-check the stored chains with obstacles dilated by it. If they fail, add mechanical datums (indexed toe rail or wall rail per station), or accept per-station touch-off and re-targeting. Most of this is laptop work (budget plus dilated re-check, about 3 days). The compliance and dock-repeatability inputs need a cheap physical rig.

**2. Tip-over and structure are screened quasi-statically with guessed masses (rows 1, 2, 4, 9, 11).** The R17 chains were stability-checked with `mass=0`. R19's 0.5 m/s² result (wok 42.7 mm) uses unsourced constants: bumper 250 N, μ 0.5, and a ×1.5 factor applied only to gravity. Arm link accelerations, protective stops and E-stops are not modelled. The reserve moment is only about 30 N·m **[est]**. Stopping a held 4.15 kg load from speed at 1.1 m height can plausibly produce 23–46 N·m before counting link inertia. The cart has no designed brakes, and no CAD to show the 14 kg chassis box is real. To close this, build a CAD/BOM mass model, run `mj_inverse` along every stored path plus UR stop cases, add a slope term, and FEA the mast, beam and adapter. A physical push and tilt test is the final gate. That is about 1–1.5 weeks on a laptop, then about 1 day of hardware.

**3. The user's L-shaped kitchen is not the simulated kitchen (rows 6, 7, 8, 21).** Everything verified sits on one straight 2.94 m counter. In reality the sink, where the wok is rinsed (a binding user decision), is on the other leg. Unmodelled steps on that route:
- undocking with a food-laden or hot wok;
- a 90° turn in an aisle of unknown width;
- re-docking against a different cabinet run;
- the tether following the turn.

The parking spot and the floor slope or thresholds (common in Singapore wet kitchens) are also unknown. This could invalidate the R17/R18 sink chains and the meal timing. To close this, survey the kitchen (1 day), then run a 2-D swept-area corner study and a MuJoCo rebuild of both legs (about 3 days). If the turn fails, revisit the rinse concept before anything else.

**4. Heat, steam and grease at the wok meet printed polymers and an IP54 arm (rows 15, 16, 17, 18).** The wok grip block is only about 6.5 cm from a rim that can reach 150–250 °C. A fin estimate puts it at 60–110 °C **[est]**. The fingers that grip it are PETG (Tg ≈80 °C) with a TPU skin. The 90 °C plume figure behind the ≥0.20 m rule is itself an estimate, and steam at lid-off and plating is unsimulated. The UR5e is IP54 yet holds the wok under a running tap and works in grease aerosol. A protective jacket would eat the 2 mm reserves. To close this, do thermal calcs and choose block and finger materials, then run a thermocouple test on a real wok and measure the plume (about 3 days, cheap hardware). Then do a splash-zone map and a jacket clearance re-check.

**5. The gripper and fixtures run at their limits without a duty or fatigue case (rows 12, 13, 14, 19).** Plating uses 6.47 of the PGC's 7 N·m *static* moment rating, with no stated reference point, about 4 times per meal. Stir strokes, about 247 per meal, see p95 grip moments of 2.2–2.9 N·m with wedging spikes of 8–25 N·m. Several load-bearing interfaces are not designed: the keyed wok slot in a probably glass-ceramic cooker bowl, the faucet clip, the lid rack lead-in and the drum holder. That leaves the 2 mm-class fixture tolerances unproven. To close this, get dynamic and fatigue ratings from DH and add a palm key that carries the moment. Run a bench cycle test with a 2.95 kg offset load. Design the rim collar and clips in CAD, then print and test them. Budget 1–2 weeks with cheap hardware.
